## Slide 1

We estimate the trace of a matrix without reading all its entries. We can only ask for matrix--vector products, and the number of those products is limited. This setting matters for operators such as neural-network Hessians, where even one product can be expensive. Hutch++ learns a useful subspace and samples the remainder. Our broader research question is whether changing that allocation can be justified reliably after accounting for its cost. I will introduce the classical estimators, explain our earlier work on statistical comparisons and their limitations, and then show a recent allocation experiment. These address complementary parts of useful adaptation; we do not yet have a cheap general online selector. Source: integrated October 19 report introduction; preserved v10 dictionary.

## Slide 2

The classical starting point is Hutchinson. Each probe has independent plus-or-minus-one entries. We query its matrix product, take the inner product, and obtain a scalar trace sample. The sample is unbiased, meaning its expectation is the trace. Averaging independent samples reduces variance, but each sample costs another query. Unbiasedness is not a guarantee that any one run is accurate. Hutch++ retains this random averaging but applies it to what remains after a low-rank subspace has been captured. Source: M.F. Hutchinson, Communications in Statistics--Simulation and Computation 19(2), 433--450 (1990), DOI 10.1080/03610919008812866; Meyer et al., Hutch++ Section 1.1, arXiv:2010.09649. Fixed symmetric matrix; independent Rademacher coordinates and probes.

## Slide 3

Hutch++ first learns a low-rank subspace, evaluates its trace contribution, and samples the residual with Hutchinson probes. The original balanced version assigns one third of the budget to each stage. The accepted basis rank is r, which need not always equal the attempted width q. Prior adaptive variants already aim at requested accuracy, so adaptation itself is not our novelty. We study how to compare allocation choices reliably when the total budget is fixed and every query counts. Changing the sketch affects both the residual and the number of samples left for final averaging. Source: Meyer, Musco, Musco and Woodruff, SOSA 2021, arXiv:2010.09649; Persson, Cortinovis and Kressner, SIAM J. Matrix Anal. Appl. 43(3), 1162--1185 (2022), arXiv:2109.10659.

## Slide 4

Here reliable refers to trusting an allocation decision. It does not mean that Hutchinson unbiasedness is in doubt. Let R a be the final mean-squared error of a proposed allocation and R zero that of the reference baseline. The question is whether their difference is negative. We freeze the choices and their final-probe counts before drawing checking probes. We also charge construction and checking, because they reduce the final-sampling budget. A smaller estimated risk is useful evidence, but it does not automatically justify switching. We need to understand the uncertainty of that comparison. Source: integrated sections certification and confidence, particularly direct risk differences and fixed-action assumptions. Baseline risk and paid fallback must not be interchanged: returning to the baseline after spending queries cannot refund those queries.

## Slide 5

We examined the actual residual observations instead of fitting only a spectral decay model. For each frozen action, a fresh probe produces a residual quadratic form. Its sample variance estimates single-probe residual variance. Dividing by the action's own remaining final-probe count estimates its final risk. Their difference is unbiased. The same fresh probe serves both actions when their matrix products are cached and charged. Pairing can reduce the variance of the risk difference when the two risk estimates have positive covariance; sharing probes alone does not guarantee that sign. An unbiased estimate remains noisy, so this is not a confidence certificate by itself. Source: integrated certification section, exact sample-variance moments and direct common-probe risk differences, Propositions T7--T8. Independent fresh final probes are separate from the checking batch.

## Slide 6

This is a mathematical calculation, not an additional experiment. Our exact sample-variance analysis identifies the fourth-moment information needed to quantify uncertainty. The conservative degree-two envelope gives actual kurtosis at most fifteen. Combining it with Chebyshev and a two-action union bound at total failure probability five percent gives the plotted relative radius. It stays above one for checking counts two through thirty-two; even at thirty-two it is about four point one nine. Inverting this particular relative bound therefore cannot give a finite candidate variance upper bound on that grid. This does not prove that all confidence methods are impossible. The report also provides a finite paired radius under frozen nested actions and a valid pre-checking residual norm bound, but a finite radius alone does not establish useful acceptance. Source: integrated confidence section, Propositions T11 and T13. The plotted formula is epsilon squared equal to two times fourteen plus two over s-minus-one, divided by s times .05.

## Slide 9

These parts address different requirements for useful adaptation. The recent experiments locate promising allocations and show how their adequacy changes with structure. The earlier analysis asks what evidence is needed to justify changing a realized allocation, and what that evidence costs. Neither result should be used as a substitute for the other. Knowing a good width exists inside a cap does not tell us which width to pick for a new matrix. Having a valid comparison bound does not show that enough improvement remains after checking. The unresolved link is an affordable decision rule using information genuinely available through matrix--vector queries. Sources: integrated report discussion; earlier certification analysis; Phase4 and V3 research conclusions. This is a synthesis of established project findings and open questions, not a new theorem or a completed estimator.

## Slide 10

The earlier work shows why reliable adaptation needs more than an unbiased risk estimate. Uncertainty and the cost of obtaining evidence both matter. The recent work shows that smaller sketches can be enough in tested regimes, but matrix structure changes which allocations are appropriate. Our immediate direction is to understand when a smaller sketch is sufficient, especially the role of spectral floor and decay. Eventually we want clues that can be obtained cheaply from a new black-box matrix, followed by a selection rule that does not spend away its own benefit. We should keep the limits explicit: most evidence is from selected synthetic matrices and budgets; no universal quarter-budget guarantee or completed online selector is established.
